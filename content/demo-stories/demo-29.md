## Chapter 1: Damsons

The damsons were late that year, which gave Maggie and her father one more week of not talking about it.

They picked on the Saturday, in the long thin orchard behind the chapel that nobody owned and everybody used. Ray held the ladder and Maggie went up, because she was lighter and because it meant they faced different directions. The fruit came off in her hands dusty blue, so dark it was nearly black, and she dropped it into the bucket hung on a hook and listened to it land.

"Your mum rang," Ray said, to the ladder.

"I know. She rang me too."

"Right."

A damson missed the bucket and went down into the grass. Neither of them went after it.

Her mother had moved to the city in June, to a flat with a balcony and a man called Stephen who worked in insurance and was, as far as Maggie could tell, perfectly nice, which was somehow the worst part. There had been no shouting. There had been a lot of cups of tea going cold on the kitchen table while adults said sentences that began with "The thing is."

"She asked if you were going to do the jam," Ray said.

"Course I'm doing the jam."

"That's what I said."

The jam was older than any of them. Ninety-nine jars, the first Sunday in September, boiled in the chapel hall on two gas rings and a camping stove, one jar for each house in Little Fenny. Nobody knew who had started it. Maggie's grandmother had run it for thirty years and then her mother for twelve, and the sheets with the recipe and the house list were in a biscuit tin in the hall cupboard, soft with handling.

"Dad," Maggie said from the top of the ladder. "There's a hundred and forty houses now."

The ladder went still.

"The new estate," she said. "On the allotments. Forty-one houses. I counted on the way to school. Do they get jam?"

Ray was quiet for a while. Below her she could see the top of his head, where the hair was going, which she had never noticed before this summer and now could not stop noticing.

"Your mother would know," he said finally.

"Mum's not here."

"No."

A wood pigeon started up in the next tree, the same four notes over and over, and Maggie picked faster, and her father held the ladder, and the bucket filled, and the question sat in the orchard between them along with the damson in the grass.

That night she got the biscuit tin out and read the recipe at the kitchen table. At the bottom, in her grandmother's slanting hand, somebody had written: *If there are more houses, make more jam. That's the whole rule.*

## Chapter 2: The Hall

By ten o'clock on Sunday the chapel hall smelled the way it smelled one day a year, of hot sugar and wet wool and the paraffin stove, and the windows had steamed over so the village outside was only shapes.

Twelve women and three men stood at the trestle tables stoning damsons with their thumbs, purple to the wrist. Maggie had the house list on a clipboard and the master pan, because that was the job her mother had done, and nobody had said anything about it, which was how she knew they had all discussed it beforehand.

"Ninety-nine," said Mrs Oakes from the shop, when Maggie counted the jars onto the side table. "Same as ever."

"We need a hundred and forty."

The stoning stopped.

"Those houses," said Mrs Oakes, "are not Little Fenny. They're the Meadows. It says so on the sign."

"They're on the allotments. The allotments were in Little Fenny."

"The allotments were allotments."

Her father, at the far table, kept stoning. He was no help at all, and Maggie wanted to throw a damson at him, and also wanted him to say something, anything, in the voice he had used when she was small and someone was unkind to her at the school gate.

The hall door opened and let in cold air and a boy her own age, in a hoodie, carrying a cardboard box with great care.

"Is this where the jam is?" he said. "My gran said there was jam."

Everyone looked at him. He had the particular stillness of someone who has walked into a room and realised too late that it has rules.

"We're from number eighteen," he said. "The Meadows. My gran's got about forty jars in the cellar from when she lived in Saltmere. She said you might want them. She said people always run out." He put the box down. "I'm Kit."

Nobody spoke. Then Ray put down his damson, wiped his hands on his apron, and crossed the hall.

"Ray Fenwick," he said, and shook the boy's hand, purple and all. "That's my daughter, Maggie. She's in charge. You'd best ask her where she wants them."

Maggie looked at her father, and her father looked back at her, and something that had been stuck all summer shifted an inch.

"On the side table," she said. "And then grab a knife. Stones go in the bowl."

Kit stoned damsons badly and cheerfully until two. Mrs Oakes said nothing further but washed every one of the Saltmere jars herself, scalding them in the big sink with her mouth set, which in Little Fenny was as good as an apology.

## Chapter 3: The Hundred and Fortieth

They ran out of damsons at jar one hundred and thirty-nine.

It was nearly dark. The hall had emptied to Maggie, her father, Kit, who had not gone home, and Mrs Oakes, who had stopped pretending she was about to. The jars stood in rows on every surface, lids on, labels drying, deep red where the lamp caught them.

"One short," Maggie said.

"It's a hundred and thirty-nine more than nobody got," said Mrs Oakes, buttoning her coat. "Goodnight, Fenwicks. Goodnight, Saltmere."

When the door shut behind her, Kit said, "Is that going to be my name now?"

"Probably," said Ray. "Sorry."

"It's all right. Better than the one at my last school."

Maggie scraped the pan. There was a spoonful left, the thick dark stuff from the bottom that never set properly and that her grandmother used to call the cook's share. She looked at it, and then at the one empty Saltmere jar, small and squat with a white lid, and she filled it. It came halfway.

"That's not a jar of jam," her father said. "That's a sample."

"It's for Mum."

Ray didn't say anything. Kit, who had good instincts, took the stoning bowls to the sink and ran the tap loudly.

"She's not on the list," Ray said at last. "She's not a house."

"She's a house. It's just not here." Maggie wrote the label in her mother's own neat capitals, the way she had been taught: DAMSON, LITTLE FENNY, SEPT. Then, below, smaller: *No. 140. If there are more houses, make more jam.*

Her father read it over her shoulder. He took his glasses off and rubbed his eyes, and put them back on, and read it again.

"We'll post it tomorrow," he said. "Bubble wrap. Your mum'll moan about the postage."

"She will."

"Go on then. Tell Saltmere he can take two for his gran. One for the cellar and one to actually eat."

They walked back through the village in the dark, the three of them, and Kit turned off at the new sign that said The Meadows, and Maggie and her father went on alone. At the gate he stopped.

"I'm not good at this," he said. "The talking."

"I know, Dad."

"I'm going to get better at it."

"I know."

A week later the phone rang while they were eating tea, and her mother said she had opened the jar standing up in the kitchen and eaten half of it with a spoon and cried on Stephen, who had not understood a word but had been very kind about it. "Next year," she said, "I'm coming for the Sunday. If that's allowed."

Maggie looked at her father across the table. He nodded.

"There's a hundred and forty houses," she told her mother. "We'll need the help."
