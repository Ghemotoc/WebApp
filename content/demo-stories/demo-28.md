## Chapter 1: The Kitchen Light

The taxi from the station wouldn't go further than the war memorial, so Nell carried her bag the last half mile up Quarry Road the way she had carried her school bag at eleven, leaning into the wind off the moor. Nothing had moved. The chip shop had a new sign and the same smell. The Methodist chapel was a carpet warehouse now, which somebody had told her years ago, and she had said "oh" and forgotten it.

Her mother's kitchen light was on. It was always on. Growing up, Nell had thought of it as a lighthouse, and later, in the city, as something more like a surveillance camera.

Her mother opened the door before she knocked.

"You'll want tea," Jean said, and turned back inside, and that was the greeting.

The kitchen was exactly itself: the brown teapot, the calendar from the feed merchant, the radio turned low so it was company without being conversation. Tam's boots were by the back door. Nell looked at them once and then at the kettle.

"When did they..." she started.

"Tuesday. The divers." Jean set two mugs down with great care, as if they might go off. "They've done what they do. The undertaker's got him. Thursday, eleven o'clock, St Mark's, then the Crown after. Dougie Hale's sorting the Crown."

"Dougie."

"He was Tam's friend. One of them."

Nell wrapped her hands round the mug. Her mother had put the milk in first, the way Nell had stopped doing at nineteen because a flatmate had laughed at it.

"Mum. How was he? Before."

Jean looked at the window. The dark pressed on the glass. "He was Tam."

That was all she would get, Nell understood. Her mother had a way of closing a subject like a drawer, with a small firm push and no sound, and you could stand there with your fingers in it or you could step back.

She stepped back. She asked about the hens, which were fine, and about Auntie Marion's hip, which was not, and at half past nine her mother said she'd made up Nell's old bed and went upstairs without saying goodnight, because she never had.

Nell sat in the kitchen for a long time. On the fridge, under a magnet shaped like a sheep, was a list in Tam's square capitals: BULBS. GATE HINGE. CAR TAX. RING NELL. The last one had no tick against it. None of them did.

She did not cry. She washed the mugs, dried them, put them back on the wrong hooks, and left the kitchen light on.

## Chapter 2: The Crown

The Crown had been done up since she left, which in Caddow meant somebody had painted the woodwork grey and put a chalkboard outside with a quote on it. Inside it was the same long room with the same fire that never quite drew, full of people in their good dark coats, eating sandwiches cut into triangles and saying "well" to each other.

Nell knew about half of them. The other half knew her, which was worse.

"You're the sister," a woman said, holding her elbow. "You're in the city."

"A city."

"Same thing," the woman said kindly, and moved on.

She found a corner by the dartboard and held a glass of something and watched her mother across the room, upright among the folding chairs, accepting condolences like a clerk accepting forms. Jean had not cried at the church either. The vicar had said Tam was "a quiet man who loved the hills," which was true and said nothing, and Nell had stared at the back of the pew and tried to make the words add up to her brother.

Dougie Hale came over at about four, when the sandwiches were gone and the drinking had changed gear. He was broader than she remembered and his eyes were red.

"Nell."

"Dougie. Thanks for sorting all this."

"Somebody had to." He looked at her glass, then at her. "He talked about you, you know. All the time. Our Nell this, our Nell that. Our Nell's got a proper job."

"I know."

"Do you?" He wasn't shouting. That was the thing. He said it level, like a man laying bricks. "Because he was in a bad way since Christmas, and everyone in this room could see it, and you'd have seen it too if you'd ever picked up the bloody phone."

The room did not go quiet. It was not that kind of room. But the man beside Dougie put a hand on his arm, and Dougie shook it off and went out the back door into the yard, and Nell stood there with the glass sweating in her hand.

"He's upset," said the woman who thought all cities were the same.

"Yes," Nell said. "So am I."

She put the glass down and walked out the front, past the chalkboard, up Quarry Road in the dark, past her mother's house with its lit kitchen, through the gap in the fence everyone used, to the edge of the lake. The water was black and absolutely still. There was a fence, new, and flowers in cellophane tied to it, already frozen.

She said his name out loud. Nothing happened. She had not expected it to. She stood there anyway until her feet stopped hurting and simply went numb, and then she went home.

## Chapter 3: Ring Nell

She found the notebook on the Friday, clearing his room because her mother wouldn't and somebody had to. It was in the drawer with his socks, a cheap thing with a cracked spine, and she nearly put it in the bag for the charity shop without opening it.

Inside were lists. Tam had always made lists; he thought in them. Jobs for the week. Things to buy. The weights he'd lifted, in the years he lifted weights. Then, from about October, something else.

*Things to do before spring.* Fix the gate. Take Mum to the sea, she hasn't been since Dad. Learn to cook one thing properly. Stop drinking on weekdays. Stop drinking. Ring Nell. Ring Nell and actually say it. Ring Nell and don't hang up when she answers.

She sat on his bed with the book open on her knees for a long time.

Downstairs, her mother was doing the thing she did with the oven, cleaning it when it did not need cleaning. Nell stood in the kitchen doorway and held up the notebook.

"He wanted to ring me."

Jean did not turn round. "Yes."

"He never did. Not once since the summer. I'd have answered, Mum. I'd have come."

"I told him not to."

The oven door closed. Jean straightened, slowly, one hand on the counter, and faced her daughter, and for the first time that week she looked seventy.

"He'd ring you drunk, and you'd hear it, and you'd worry, and you had your work. I said, wait till you're right, Tam, then ring her. Wait till you've something good to say. I thought there was time." Her voice did not break. It simply got smaller. "I thought there was always time. There's always been time, in this house. Everything waits."

Nell put the notebook on the table between them. Neither of them touched it.

"That was a shit thing to do," she said.

"Yes."

"To both of us."

"Yes."

They stood there. The radio murmured about weather coming in from the west. Outside the window the light was going, and the hens were making their evening fuss, and the gate at the end of the yard swung on its one hinge and knocked the post, the way it had knocked all week.

"I'm going to fix that gate tomorrow," Nell said.

"You don't know how."

"Then you can tell me."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment, and then nodded, once, and reached for the kettle. She filled it, put it on, and took down two mugs, and put the milk in first, and Nell let her.

She stayed through the winter. The gate held. In March they drove to the sea, the two of them, and sat in the car in the rain with the windows steaming up and did not say much, and it was enough, or it was a start.
