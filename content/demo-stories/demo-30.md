## Chapter 1: The Rule

There was one rule at Quietside Station that mattered more than all the others, and it was painted on the inside of the airlock in letters as tall as Ilse's forearm: NO TRANSMISSIONS.

Everything else followed from it. The station sat in a crater on the far side of the Moon because the Moon itself, three and a half thousand kilometres of rock, stood between the great dish and the noise of Earth. Every phone, every satellite, every microwave oven on the home planet was a scream the astronomers had come here not to hear. Inside the crater the sky was quiet in a way nowhere else in the solar system was quiet, and the dish listened to things older than light.

Ilse had lived here since she was eleven. Her mother ran the array. Her father had stayed on Earth, which was a story that fitted in one sentence and did not need telling again.

She was fifteen now, the only person under thirty-eight within a hundred thousand kilometres, and she knew every corridor, every panel, every sound the station made when it breathed. She knew that Dr Okafor hummed when he was worried and that Petra in Life Support kept a cat video on a loop she thought nobody had seen. She knew the schedule of the relay satellite that passed overhead twice a day and carried their data home in a tight, encrypted burst, the only legal signal that ever left the crater.

And she knew, because she had built it, exactly where the second transmitter was.

It lived in Locker 14-C, behind the spare water filters, in a box that had once held a centrifuge. It was not much: a board scavenged from a dead sensor, a coil she had wound by hand over three weeks, an antenna that was really a length of cable fed up an unused conduit to the surface. It could reach, on a good day, about as far as the limb of the Moon, where the far side turned into the near side and the quiet ended.

That was far enough. On the near side, at a mining station called Prospect, there was someone called Jun who was sixteen and had built almost the same thing for almost the same reason, and who answered.

They talked at night, in short bursts, when the dish was pointed away and the station slept. They talked about nothing, which was the point. Jun's dog on Earth. The taste of real bread. Whether the stars looked different from a crater than from a mine.

Ilse told herself the signal was too weak to matter. She told herself this every night, in the dark, with the headset warm against her ear, and every night she believed it a little less.

## Chapter 2: Jun

"Say again, Quietside. You're breaking up."

"I said my mother's going to find it. The transmitter. She does an inventory of the lockers every quarter and the quarter ends Thursday."

A pause, full of the hiss of the Moon. Then Jun's voice, flattened by distance and a bad speaker, but still unmistakably Jun, who laughed before the end of sentences.

"So move it."

"Where? It's a station, not a house. There's no under the bed."

"Prospect has under the bed. Prospect has a whole level nobody's been in since the first shaft closed. Half the lights still work."

Ilse lay on the floor of the locker room with the headset on and her boots against the door so she would feel it if anyone tried to come in. Above her, through four metres of regolith shielding, the sky was doing what it always did, which was nothing, enormously.

"Jun. Do you ever think about what happens if they catch us?"

"Every day."

"What happens?"

"At Prospect? My dad shouts, I lose network for a month, life goes on. We're a mine. Nobody cares about a bit of noise." The hiss rose and fell. "At your place it's different, isn't it."

"At my place it's the whole reason the place exists."

She had not said that out loud before. It sat there between them, bounced off the limb of the Moon.

"Then why'd you build it?" Jun asked. Not unkindly.

Ilse looked at the ceiling. She thought about the first winter here, when the sun went down for fourteen days and everyone worked and she did lessons alone on a tablet and the loudest sound in the whole world was her own breathing in her own helmet during the one hour of surface time a week.

"Because I wanted to hear a voice that wasn't a scientist," she said. "Because the quiet is the point for them and for me it's just quiet."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's selfish. The data they're getting, Jun. They're listening to the first few hundred million years of everything. And I'm leaking pop songs into it."

"You've never sent me a pop song."

"I've thought about it."

He laughed, and the laugh came in pieces, and she put them together in her head.

"Thursday," he said. "Move it Wednesday night. Tell me where you put it. If you go quiet for more than a week I'll assume you got caught and I'll start sending Morse code into your dish until somebody notices."

"Don't you dare."

"Then don't get caught, Quietside."

The relay satellite would be over in six minutes. She signed off, coiled the cable, closed the centrifuge box, and lay for a while longer in the dark, listening to the station breathe, and did not move the transmitter on Wednesday, because on Wednesday the lights went out.

## Chapter 3: The Long Dark

It was not dramatic. That was what she remembered afterwards. No alarm, no shudder. The corridor lights simply went from white to amber, which meant the station had dropped to battery, and then a quarter of them went off altogether, which meant the station was choosing.

Ilse was in the galley. Dr Okafor was humming.

"Main bus," he said, to no one. "That's the main bus."

By the time she reached Operations, half the crew was there, in the strange blue light of screens running on reserve power. Her mother stood at the centre of it with her arms folded and her face set in the shape it took when she was doing sums she did not like.

"Regulator failure in the primary array," Dr Okafor said. "The solar farm's fine. It's charging a battery we can't draw from. We're on reserve."

"How long?"

"Sixty hours. Fifty if we keep the dish warm. We can't let the dish freeze."

"Relay?"

"Next pass in nineteen hours. We can send the fault report then and Earth can send a parts manifest and the supply hop from Prospect takes thirty hours if they're ready to go." He did not say the rest, so Ilse said it in her head: nineteen plus thirty is forty-nine, and fifty if we keep the dish warm, and that is one hour, and this is a station with twelve people in it.

Nobody panicked. Scientists did not panic; they re-ran the numbers. Her mother divided the crew into shifts. Petra went to drain the lines that could be drained. Ilse was sent to her cabin "to keep warm and stay out of the way," and she went, and sat on her bunk in her parka, and watched her breath begin to show.

Nineteen hours.

Prospect was eight hundred kilometres away, just over the limb. Prospect had a shuttle. Prospect had a sixteen-year-old with a radio who checked the frequency every night at twenty-two hundred, station time, which was in four hours.

If she told them, the transmitter was gone and so was she. Not from the Moon; from this. From being trusted with the airlock code and the surface rota and the quiet, grown-up conversations in the galley at night where people forgot she was fifteen. Her mother's face would do a thing she had seen it do once, on Earth, and never wanted to see again.

If she didn't tell them, it was nineteen hours they did not have.

She sat with it for twenty minutes, which felt like a long time and was not. Then she walked to Operations, past the amber lights, and stood in the doorway until her mother looked up.

"I need to show you something in Locker 14-C," she said. "And then you're going to be angry. But it can talk to Prospect right now."

## Chapter 4: Open Channel

Her mother did not shout. She did something worse, which was to go completely still for three seconds, looking at the centrifuge box, and then say, "Show me how it works," in the voice she used for instruments.

Ilse showed her. Her hands shook on the coil and she steadied them.

"Range?"

"To the limb. Prospect's inside it. I've... tested."

"I'm sure you have." Her mother looked at her, one long look that Ilse could not read and would spend years trying to. Then she turned to Dr Okafor. "Get me the station director at Prospect. If this child's radio can reach them, I want to know why our budget can't."

Jun answered first. Of course he did. It was twenty-one fifty and he was early, and his voice came through the bad speaker into a locker room full of astronomers, saying, "Quietside, you there? Thought you were moving house," and Ilse closed her eyes.

"Prospect, this is Quietside Station, Chief Scientist Halvorsen," her mother said into the handset, perfectly level. "We have a power emergency. I need your director on this channel in the next five minutes. Can you do that?"

A silence. Then, very small: "Yes, ma'am."

"Thank you. And Prospect?"

"Ma'am?"

"Good antenna."

The shuttle left Prospect four hours later with a regulator in a crate. It landed at hour thirty-one. The dish did not freeze. The data that had been queued for nineteen hours went up with the relay pass and nobody on Earth ever knew how close it had come.

The transmitter was dismantled the next day in front of the whole crew, which was the punishment, and Ilse lost her surface time for two months, which was the other punishment. She did not argue. She had known the price when she walked into Operations, and the thing about knowing the price is that you cannot be surprised by it afterwards.

But her mother came to her cabin that night and sat on the end of the bunk, something she had not done since Earth.

"You broke the one rule," she said.

"I know."

"Then you broke it again to save twelve people. Which is also a rule. It's just not painted on anything." She looked at her hands. "I built a radio when I was fourteen. My father took it apart with a hammer. I'm not going to do that. I'm going to make you file a proposal."

"A what?"

"A low-power licensed channel to Prospect, scheduled, logged, outside observing windows. You'll write it up properly. If it's good enough, I'll sign it." She stood up. "It had better be very good."

It was. It took Ilse three weeks and two rewrites. The first legal transmission from Quietside Station to Prospect went out at twenty-two hundred on a Thursday, and was logged, and was mostly about a dog.
